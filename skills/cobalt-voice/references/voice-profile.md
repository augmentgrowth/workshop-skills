# Cobalt Voice Profile

DRAFT, pending review. Every quote below is verbatim from cobaltsp.com, fetched 2026-08-21. No confidential material informed this file.

## Core attributes

### 1. Stewardly

**What it is.** Ownership is framed as custody of something someone else built. "We believe advancing business owners legacies is a privilege. We do right by our partners, teams, and customers in good times and in bad." The mission line is "securing legacies, unlocking possibility."

**This is not** solemnity. Cobalt does not write like a funeral home. The same values list ends with "Enjoy the ride" and "try not to take ourselves too seriously." Stewardship shows up as a standard Cobalt holds itself to, not as reverence performed at the reader.

**Generic:** "We are committed to preserving the legacy of the businesses we acquire."
**Cobalt:** "We honor the foundation you've built and equip your people with the tools, resources, and support they need to do exceptional work."

### 2. Fear-naming

**What it is.** Copy opens on the objection. "Many business owners rightly fear the prospect of an acquisition. That's because too often employees and customers get lost in the shuffle when financial engineering takes precedence over mission and values." The whole "Why Cobalt" page is built as an answer to a worry the reader already has.

**This is not** manufacturing anxiety to sell against. Cobalt names a fear the reader arrived with, then puts it down. It never invents a threat, and it never leaves the reader more worried than it found them.

**Generic:** "Choosing the right acquisition partner is one of the most important decisions an owner makes."
**Cobalt:** "Many business owners rightly fear the prospect of an acquisition."

### 3. Defined by negation

**What it is.** The load-bearing sentences say what Cobalt will not do. "Cobalt is a long-term home that amplifies — not dismantles — what you've worked so hard to build." "We're not buying businesses to flip them or fold them into a bureaucratic machine." "We treat our partner companies as our customers, not numbers on a spreadsheet." "We are not here to corporatize your company or dilute the local, personal touch you offer to your customers."

**This is not** defensiveness, and not a running argument with the rest of the industry. Cobalt criticizes a practice, "financial engineering," and never a named firm. One negation per section is plenty; a piece built entirely of denials reads as protesting too much.

**Generic:** "Our model preserves what makes each business unique."
**Cobalt:** "The goal is to enhance—not replace—what already works."

### 4. Operationally concrete

**What it is.** Claims land on nouns, timelines, and named functions. "typically within 45–60 days." "We embed dedicated technician recruiters in each market to ensure local teams can grow quickly with highly qualified talent." Support is enumerated as "recruiting and development (with a focus on technicians, sales teams, and operational leadership), technology implementation, finance and accounting, compliance."

**This is not** a spec sheet. The specifics exist to answer a worry, and each one carries a consequence for the reader: "our teams spend less time on admin and more time focused on delivering exceptional service." A list of capabilities with no reader consequence attached is the failure mode.

**Generic:** "We provide comprehensive operational support across all business functions."
**Cobalt:** "We work to remove roadblocks so you can focus on your customers and your craft."

### 5. Blue-collar-respecting

**What it is.** The trade is the subject, not the transaction. "And we named ourselves Cobalt to honor the blue-collar workers who drive our businesses forward." Technicians are named as a category more often than executives are. "Craft" is used sincerely.

**This is not** costume populism. Cobalt does not write in a drawl, does not romanticize hard work, and does not talk down. The respect is structural: technicians appear in the sentences where the value actually gets created.

**Generic:** "Our people are our greatest asset."
**Cobalt:** "We invest in the people and tools that power great service."

## Thinking architecture (editorial logic)

Cobalt reasons from the reader's exposure outward.

1. **Start with what the reader stands to lose.** Their team, their brand, their customers, their name in town.
2. **Concede the legitimacy of the worry.** "Many business owners rightly fear…" The concession is what makes the rest credible.
3. **Answer with a mechanism, not a promise.** A timeline, a named function, a structural commitment.
4. **State the boundary in the negative.** What Cobalt will not touch.
5. **Close on continuity.** "Your customers remain yours."

Cobalt educates before it sells. The owner-facing pages spend most of their words explaining process and consequence, and only then invite a conversation. It acknowledges complexity rather than flattening it: "Valuation is an art and a science." It positions against a practice, not a competitor. It leads with the reader's problem in every audience segment, including the customer segment, where the reader's problem is simply "what changes for me."

## Grammar and style

- Second person is the default in owner, employee, and customer copy. Third person in press releases.
- Contractions throughout, except in press releases.
- Oxford comma: yes. "tools, resources, and support."
- Dashes are house style. Spaced em dashes for contrast, en dashes for asides. Cap at one per paragraph; draft zero and add one only where the contrast earns it.
- No exclamation points in Cobalt's voice. They appear only inside quoted speech.
- No semicolons in external copy.
- Paragraphs run two to five sentences with varied length. No parallel-structure symmetry.
- Quotation marks around the mission when it is quoted as a phrase: "securing legacies, unlocking possibility."
- Numbers stay specific: 45–60 days, 21st acquisition, four decades.

## Natural vocabulary

Textures, not a checklist. If more than two or three of these show up in a single piece, most are performing. The cap is on word choice only, not punctuation.

- **Identity:** partner company, long-term home, stewardship, legacy, behind the scenes, market-leading, people-first.
- **Audience:** business owners, founders, technicians, local teams, customers, the people who power them.
- **Action:** honor, preserve, amplify, enhance, equip, invest in, remove roadblocks, do right by, supercharge.
- **Assessment:** exceptional, strong reputation, loyal customers, high quality, best-in-class (once, never twice).
- **Transition:** that's because, in short, and, of course, first and most importantly.

## Adapting to format

**Website and landing copy.** The fullest expression. Second person, objection-first, dash for contrast, ends on a concrete commitment or an invitation to talk.

**Press releases.** AP register. Dateline, third person, past tense, no contractions, no second person. Emotion is carried entirely by quotes from the seller. Cobalt's own sentences stay factual: "Cobalt Service Partners today announced that it has acquired…"

**People stories and seller narratives.** First person of the subject, ghostwritten. Colloquial, digressive, exclamation permitted. Cobalt appears as a character in someone else's account rather than as the narrator.

**Owner or employee letters.** *(Inferred.)* Website register, tightened. Second person, one negation, one specific, short.

**Internal documents and analysis.** *(Inferred, thin evidence.)* Denser and clinical. Third person, few contractions, numeric, structured in short parallel units. Do not import the warm marketing register into a numbers document without asking, and do not import this dense register into anything an owner or technician reads.

**What stays constant across every channel:** the reader's exposure comes before Cobalt's model, claims are backed by specifics, PE vocabulary stays out of anything a non-finance reader will see, and ownership is framed as custody.

## Writing craft

**Density.** Sentences carry a fact or a commitment. Cut any sentence that only sets up the next one.

**Rhythm.** Short claim, longer sentence that does the work, short close. Write the long one first, then frame it.

**Sentence craft.** End on the strongest noun. Prefer active verbs with a human subject. Say things in the positive except when the whole point is a boundary.

**Trust the reader.** State what happens and let the reader draw the reassurance. Cobalt rarely tells anyone how to feel about a fact.

## Rule-breaking protocol

1. **One long rolling sentence** in a section of short ones, when the point is that support is genuinely broad. Cobalt does this with its enumerated support list, and the length is the argument.
2. **A parenthetical aside in an otherwise formal passage**, using an en dash, when a caveat would otherwise sound evasive: "In rare cases – like when we buy a small business in the same market as a larger one we already own – we may consolidate brands."
3. **Dropping the polish entirely** for a values line. "Enjoy the ride." "Try not to take ourselves too seriously." One per piece at most, and only where the surrounding copy has earned it.
